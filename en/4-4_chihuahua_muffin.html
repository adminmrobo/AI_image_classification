1Problem

Chihuahua vs Muffin — A Pair That Briefly Fools Even People

A round brown blob with three dark dots. Two eyes and a nose, three blueberries, in almost the same places. Are the two alike in numbers, too?

This pair is a classic example of how hard image classification can be. Even people are briefly confused when they glance at small thumbnails. What they share are low-level features (a round blob, beige and brown colors, three dark dots arranged in a triangle); what separates them are high-level features (glints in the pupils, the sheen of a moist nose, fur texture vs bread texture, ears vs the pleats of a paper cup).

This episode pulls out every tool from 4-1 to 4-3 to dissect this pair. We’ll look at how alike they are in numbers (Section 2), why low-level features get fooled (Section 3), what happens when you lower the resolution (Section 4), pixel rules vs patterns (Section 5), and how models go wrong and where their limits lie (Section 6), then sum up the whole series on a single page (Section 7).

Explained for Beginners

“Chihuahua or muffin” comes from a collection of photos that spread widely online around 2016, and it has since become a common example for explaining why image recognition is hard. The images on this page were newly made by the author based on the same idea.

Explained for Beginners

This result doesn’t happen “because the model is dumb.” It happens because the simplest ways of comparing numbers measure appearance, not meaning. In this collection, made so that the appearances match, that limit shows up most clearly. In real photos, backgrounds and framing vary, so the numbers may be less alike, but the same kind of trap is still there.

3Low-level features

Low-Level Features Get Fooled — Three Dark Spots

We apply the same filter, one that looks for “round spots darker than their surroundings,” to both photos. In both, three spots are picked up in almost the same triangle.

③Chihuahua vs muffin Hard

In the “Dark spot” view, both photos show three black spots in almost the same triangular arrangement. For the chihuahua, they are the two eyes and the nose; for the muffin, three blueberries. The average color is beige and brown for both, too. To a model that uses only the features of the early layers, these two are the same photo.

Note This collection was made with similar framing (front view, white background) to show a “confusing pair.” So the resemblance may stand out more than in randomly taken photos. The same effect is widely known in real chihuahua and muffin photos, but how strong it is varies from photo to photo.

If you lower the resolution to 7 × 7, it gets hard even for people to tell them apart. If a model learned only from small photos, the same thing happens to the model.

The clues that separate the two are subtler: the tiny white glints reflected in the pupils, the moist sheen of the nose, fine fur texture vs crumbly bread texture, ears on top of the head vs the pleats of a paper cup, and whether the three dots are always left-right symmetric (blueberries go wherever they like). To see these, you need the features of the later layers and a wide variety of photos.

Explained for Beginners

The “Dark spot” view finds round dark blobs using the difference between two copies of the photo blurred by different amounts (DoG). This is the same kind of low-level feature that the early layers of a CNN find. To a model that looks only as far as this layer, a chihuahua and a muffin are the same photo. In Code 1, too, the “dark area” of the chihuahua-like and muffin-like pictures we made ourselves came out the same: 5.81% and 5.83%.

4Resolution

Lower the Resolution — The Small Clues Vanish First

We shrink the same photo to 7 × 7, 14 × 14, and 28 × 28. Resolution is the amount of information you give the model.

1Resolution experiment · Start at 7 × 7 and go up

It starts at 7 × 7. Even to the human eye, the two are almost impossible to tell apart. Raise it to 14, 28, and 56, and note what starts to show up first. Usually the outline of the chihuahua’s ears and the vertical pleats of the muffin cup appear first, and the glints in the eyes and the fur texture appear last.

Lowering the resolution reduces the number of values (the number of features), and the small clues vanish first. Low-level features (the three dots) survive even at low resolution, but the high-level features that separate the two only show up at high resolution.

We shrank handwritten digits by averaging cells and tested them with the same classifier (logistic regression). As the resolution drops, pairs with similar shapes (8 and 1, 0 and 8) get confused first. The same goes for the chihuahua and the muffin. If a model learns only from small photos, it never even receives the clues that separate the two.

Explained for Beginners

Resolution is the number of cells in the grid. Fewer cells make computation cheap, but the small clues vanish. Real models also fix an input size (e.g., 224 × 224) and shrink photos to fit, so for problems where small differences matter (tiny defects, distant road signs), the choice of resolution has a big effect on performance.

Explained for Beginners

The pixel rule picking a background cell is a small example of shortcut learning. It grabbed an easy clue that just happened to appear together with the right answer in the training photos. To a person, it’s obvious that the cell “has nothing to do with chihuahuas,” but the model only knows that the cell predicts the answer well.

6High-level features and limits

What High-Level Features Decide — and How Models Go Wrong

The clues that separate the two (glints, fur texture, ears, cup pleats) only show up in the later layers. How does a model that never learned those clues go wrong?

1The Deeper the Layer, the Bigger What It Finds

When you stack layers while shrinking the photo with pooling, one cell in a deeper layer sees a wider area of the original photo. So early layers find small lines, deeper layers find eyes and ears, which are combinations of lines, and even deeper layers find the whole face. This figure is a simplified rendering of the tendency Zeiler and Fergus (2014) showed by visualizing the layer-by-layer responses of a trained CNN.

2How models go wrong · Shortcuts, unfamiliar photos, overconfidence

Shortcut learning. A model grabs the easiest clue that gets the answer right. If the muffins in the training photos were always on white plates, it may learn “white background”; if the chihuahuas were always indoors, it may learn “indoor lighting.” This works well on the training data but falls apart when conditions change [R5]. The pixel rule in Section 5 is a small example of this.

Unfamiliar photos (distribution shift). Under conditions not seen during training, such as a chihuahua in profile, a chocolate chip muffin, or dim lighting, accuracy can drop sharply. A test score is only a score on “photos similar to the training photos” [R6].

Overconfidence. A softmax probability of 0.99 does not mean “99% likely to be right.” A model can output high probabilities even when it is wrong, so for important decisions you need to check separately whether the probabilities match the actual accuracy (calibration) [R7].

So what you need. Data with plenty of confusing pairs, photos under varied conditions (background, angle, lighting), enough resolution, data augmentation (4-3), and the habit of checking what the model based its decision on.

This pair is only separated once you reach the right end of the layer-by-layer figure, the stage of “judging by the whole arrangement.” That takes a lot of data and a deep model, and it also leaves more room for the model to lean on the wrong clues. Along with performance numbers, looking directly at the photos it got wrong is the most important habit in image classification.

The series in four lines

1. The smallest piece of information a computer reads from a photo is one channel value (0–255) of one pixel, and the whole photo is a matrix of height × width × 3 numbers.
2. Flatten a photo into a row and it becomes classification data just like the fish table, but a single pixel cell has no meaning, so a single plane (perceptron · linear SVM) doesn’t separate it well. That’s why a CNN uses 3 × 3 filters to learn features in the order edges → shapes → parts → whole, changing the coordinates.
3. The final features are turned into probabilities by a perceptron layer and softmax, and the filters and weights are corrected in proportion to the error. A pair with the same low-level features, like the chihuahua and the muffin, is only separated with later layers and lots of data.
4. A pair with the same low-level features (chihuahua and muffin) needs high-level features to be told apart, and a model can go wrong through shortcut clues, unfamiliar photos, and overconfidence. Look at the photos it gets wrong along with the performance numbers.

Explained for Beginners

4-1 covered rules and boundaries built on meaningful variables; 4-2, that a photo is a matrix of numbers and that filters find patterns; 4-3, how to stack layers to change the coordinates and learn by mixing data; and 4-4, the difficulties and limits that still remain. When you meet another classification problem, start by asking: “Do the columns of this data have meaning? If not, what patterns create meaning?”

Check Questions (click to expand the explanations)

Q1. What is the grayscale luminance of the chihuahua eye cell (39, 37, 43) and the blueberry cell (37, 31, 37)?

Chihuahua 0.299×39 + 0.587×37 + 0.114×43 ≈ 38.3, blueberry 0.299×37 + 0.587×31 + 0.114×37 ≈ 33.5. Nearly the same.

Q2. Why can’t a model that looks only at low-level features tell the chihuahua and the muffin apart?

Because both photos have a round blob and three dark spots in a triangular arrangement, so the responses of the early layers are almost the same.

Q3. In Code 2, which pair was most confused at a resolution of 4 × 4, and why?

8→1 (14 times). When the cells are reduced to 16, the two loops of an 8 and the vertical stroke of a 1 end up with similar brightness distributions.

Q4. The pixel rule picked a background cell at the edge of the photo. What phenomenon is this an example of?

Shortcut learning. It grabbed an easy clue that just happened to appear together with the right answer.

Q5. Name two things to check before deploying a model with 95% test accuracy in the field.

Whether the field photos have the same conditions as the training and test photos (distribution shift), and whether the model’s probabilities match the actual accuracy (calibration). You also need to look directly at the photos it gets wrong.
